Ryne McCormick 3. Verify Internet connectivity Growing up with dial-up internet, I learned how to troubleshoot and diagnose computer and internet connectivity issues from a young age. The connectivity lights are a good place to start on your modem or router, and being able to read them correctly is key. After checking the adapter, the next step is to see if anything else connected to the network is receiving internet, and if so, then move on to the computer having issues. If it's a wired connection I'd check to make sure that all cables are fully connected, and unseat and reseat any plugs. If wireless, I would run the diagnostic troubleshooter for Windows network problems, and that usually takes care of DNS issues. Other tools like ipconfig can help show more information and let you look up your IP address or show the route taken by the internet to reach you, looking for any issues as it traces the route to the host. 5. Research and compare license and support costs for Windows, macOS, and Linux Windows licenses for home run about $130, and are good for the life of the machine, so pretty much a one-time purchase. macOS comes with the mac computer so costs nothing up front, but the costs for the OS are rolled into the costs of the machine itself and the software. Linux is free and community driven and supported. There are paid subscription models but they are optional. As far as support costs, both Windows and Apple have paid tiers of support for users professionally and personally, but Windows rolls out updates for its operating systems for free regularly, and continues supporting old versions of the OS after the new one is released. Apple stops rolling out updates for older versions of their OS and chooses to force everyone to update or be abandoned. Linux is still community supported and driven, free to download and update always. 7.Research, identify and describe the necessary components required to set-up a home network for yourself To set up a home network, I need an ISP to provide the internet, a modem to convert the ISP signal into usable ethernet, and a router to distribute the internet to my devices. Home internet typically doesn't need more than 300 Mbps for casual users, however gigabit internet is available in some areas if you are running a business or streaming all the time. This is what you establish with an ISP or Internet Service Provider. They provide you a modem that receives their signal, and it gets converted into usable internet and broadcast through a router which assigns all of your devices unique addresses to use separately. A strong password for the access point is recommended, as well as a secure non-descript name. With all of these components installed, you are ready to surf the information highway!
